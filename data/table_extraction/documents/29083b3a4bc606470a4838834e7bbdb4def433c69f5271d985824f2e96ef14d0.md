# Metastatic lung adenocarcinoma mimicking Richter transformation in a patient with chronic lymphocytic leukemia

**Authors:** Hua-Jay J. Cherng, Nitin Jain, Beenu Thakral, Tariq Muzzafar, Roberto N. Miranda, Dongfeng Tan, Asif Rashid, Neda Kalhor, Andrew W. Hahn, Lauren A. Byers, Christine M. Parseghian, Alessandra Ferrajoli, Naveen Pemmaraju

**Published in:** Leukemia research (2020)

Patients with chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) develop second primary malignancies, including solid tumors, at higher frequencies than the general population.^1,2^ Additionally, they are at risk for developing Richter transformation (RT), most commonly as diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL). Typically, in the setting of transformation, patients present with constitutional symptoms, hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy on positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan, and “large neoplastic cells” on histopathologic examination.^3^ Herein, we describe a 66-year-old man with untreated CLL who presented with suspicion for RT. Unexpectedly, pathological evaluation of his bone marrow biopsy showed focal involvement by metastatic, poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma of the lung together with extensive involvement by CLL. This case underscores the importance of tissue biopsy confirmation in patients with suspected RT as a disease process distinct from their underlying CLL can also occur.

The patient was diagnosed with CLL 6 years prior to presentation. Prognostic markers included unmutated *IGHV* and no expression of CD38 and ZAP-70. Baseline cytogenetics were not performed at diagnosis. As the patient was asymptomatic from his disease with no associated cytopenias or lymphadenopathy, he was followed with active surveillance. One month prior to his presentation, he was seen in routine follow-up and exhibited worsening fatigue, enlarging lymph nodes, and a 30-lb weight loss. He had recently quit smoking after 40-pack-years. A PET/CT scan at an outside institution showed widespread multi-focal hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy and extra-nodal disease with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 12.7. A bone marrow biopsy was diagnosed with 90% involvement by CLL and a concurrent right axillary lymph node biopsy showed CLL with a Ki-67 of 10–20% and no sheets of large cells. Given the high concern for RT despite the initial negative workup, the patient was transferred to our institution for further work-up and management.

On presentation, physical exam was notable for a palpable 2 centimeter right axillary lymph node, bilateral subcentimeter cervical lymphadenopathy, and a distended abdomen. Complete blood count showed marked leukocytosis with lymphocytosis with WBC of 138.8 × 10^9^/L (93% lymphocytes on differential), hemoglobin of 9.4 g/dL, and platelet count of 151 × 10^9^/L. Serum potassium was 6.2 mEq/L (reference range 3.5–5.1), phosphorous 5.5 mg/dL (range 2.5–4.5), uric acid 10.1 mg/dL (range 3.4–7.0), and creatinine 2.08 mg/dL (range 0.67–1.17) consistent with spontaneous tumor lysis syndrome. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was only slightly elevated at 245 U/L (range 135–225). The patient received intravenous fluids and treatment for his hyperkalemia and hyperuricemia, leading to normalization of his renal function and other laboratory parameters.

A repeat PET/CT scan performed at our institution demonstrated multi-focal 18^F^flurodeoxyglucose (FDG)-avid lymphadenopathy. Notably there were also numerous FDG-avid discrete osseous lesions and omental masses (Figure 1A). A bone marrow biopsy and omental lymph node biopsy were performed. The bone marrow biopsy showed focal involvement by large neoplastic cells and extensive infiltrate by small, mature lymphocytes (Figure 1B). Immunohistochemical (IHC) stains showed large neoplastic cells positive for pancytokeratin and negative for PAX-5 supporting carcinoma (Figure 1C). The background neoplastic small B-cell infiltrate showed positive dual staining for PAX-5/CD5 (Figure 1D). Flow cytometric immunophenotype demonstrated the aberrant B-cells were CD5+, CD19+, CD20 dim, CD22 dim, CD23+, CD43+, CD200+, and monoclonal immunoglobulin kappa light chain dim, consistent with CLL. Molecular and cytogenetic analysis was notable for unmutated *IGHV, BIRC3* mutation, and deletion of 11q and 13q. Biopsy of the omental lymph node showed a neoplastic cell infiltrate positive for cytokeratin 7 (CK7), TTF-1, and NapsinA (Figure 1E–G) and negative for cytokeratin 20 (CK20), CDX2, SOX10, chromogranin, and PAX-8, consistent with poorly differentiated lung adenocarcinoma. A small focus of CLL was also noted. In both the specimens, no support for DLBCL or RT was seen. Despite the absence of a primary lung mass and the atypical distribution of disease with significant mesenteric involvement, based on the IHC profile, the patient was felt to have metastatic adenocarcinoma of lung origin together with CLL. Unfortunately, he rapidly decompensated before initiation of treatment and was transitioned to comfort measures, expiring in the hospital.

RT is characterized by the onset of constitutional symptoms, elevated LDH, and hypermetabolic lymphadenopathy on PET/CT scan, representing an aggressive transformation of CLL that occurs in around 5–10% of patients.^3,4^ Transformation can happen at any point during the disease course of the underlying CLL,^5^ though specific clinical factors, such as greater number of prior therapies and lymph node volume, and biological factors, such as deletion of 11q and 17p and *NOTCH1* mutation, have been associated with greater risk for RT.^3,4^ PET/CT imaging must be used in conjunction with, but cannot replace histopathologic diagnosis in confirmation of RT. In our group’s experience of 332 CLL cases, we demonstrated that an SUVmax of ≥5 was sensitive but not specific for histologically confirmed RT, and an SUVmax of ≥10 was associated with shorter overall survival in patients with RT.^6^ Our group and others have shown that RT can be mimicked by other etiologies, such as disseminated infections.^7–9^ Separately, an increased incidence of second primary malignancies, including lung cancer, has been observed in CLL patients,^1,2,10^ despite a lack of strong correlation between smoking and CLL risk.^11^ Whether this association is related to immune dysregulation,^12^ inherited polymorphisms affecting metabolism of carcinogens,^13^ or other unknown factors, this case highlights the importance of recognizing that non-hematologic second malignancies are a major cause of mortality in this patient population. This may be potentially mitigated by appropriate cancer prevention and screening strategies. Nineteen percent of CLL patient deaths could be attributed to other cancers in a prospective cohort study on 1143 patients as shown by Strati and colleagues.^14^

In summary, we describe a patient with CLL and metastatic lung adenocarcinoma involving the bone marrow and mesentery mimicking RT in clinical presentation. RT must be histopathologically confirmed even if clinical suspicion for transformation is high, as other etiologies such as second primary cancers and infections can have similar presentations. Particular care in ruling out other causes must be taken when the distribution of FDG-avid lesions is atypical for RT, like in this case where osseous and omental disease was prominent.
